**The crux of this part:** if a technique doesn't tell you the tactic by itself, what does? And given a pile of raw log evidence instead of a tidy incident report, how do you get from "something happened" to a specific, defensible ATT&CK mapping without just picking whichever tactic sounds right?

## T1078: The Technique That Breaks the Grid

Jordan's frustration is a reasonable first reaction to [T1078, Valid Accounts](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1078/). Checked directly against the current ATT&CK page, it's listed under four tactics simultaneously:

| Tactic | Why valid credentials serve it |
|---|---|
| **Initial Access** | A stolen or purchased password gets the adversary into the environment in the first place, no exploit required. |
| **Persistence** | An account that stays valid is access that survives a reboot, a patch, or an EDR sweep, unlike a dropped implant. |
| **Privilege Escalation** | The same mechanism, compromising a more privileged account instead of a regular one, moves the adversary up instead of just in. |
| **Stealth** | Authenticating with real credentials doesn't trip the alerts a malicious binary would. It looks like Tuesday. |

(Stealth is worth a flag here before Part 3 covers it properly: this used to be a single tactic called Defense Evasion. MITRE split it into Stealth and Defense Impairment in the April 2026 release, and T1078 landed in Stealth specifically, not Defense Impairment, because using a real password to blend in is categorically different from disabling the logging that would have caught it. More on that split next part.)

The thing Jordan's missing isn't a flaw in ATT&CK, it's that **a technique describes a capability, and a tactic describes what a specific adversary used that capability for, on a specific occasion.** T1078 doesn't commit to a tactic because the behavior itself, authenticating with valid credentials, doesn't commit to an objective. The same key opens whichever door the adversary points it at. What tells you which tactic applies isn't the technique page, it's the evidence: what was the account used to accomplish, *this time*?

That reframes Jordan's coverage-layer problem rather than solving it for free. You don't get to mark "T1078 covered" once. You have to ask whether your telemetry catches valid-account abuse used for initial entry, separately from whether it catches valid-account abuse used to maintain access after an incident response team thinks they've evicted the adversary. Those are different detections even though they're the same `T####` ID.

## From Raw Evidence to a Mapping: Scattered Spider at a Help Desk

Here's where that distinction stops being abstract. In November 2023, CISA and the FBI published a joint advisory, [AA23-320A](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2023-11/aa23-320a_scattered_spider.pdf), documenting Scattered Spider's TTPs from active FBI investigations. Strip the incident down to what was actually observed, in order, the way an analyst would actually encounter it, not as a pre-labeled technique list:

1. An IT help desk receives a phone call or SMS from someone claiming to be an employee, usually with enough real personal details (gathered beforehand) to sound legitimate, asking for a password reset or MFA re-enrollment.
2. The help desk, following a normal-looking process, resets the credential or re-enrolls the MFA device, now pointed at infrastructure the adversary controls.
3. In other documented cases, rather than a help desk, the adversary already has a valid password (phished or purchased) and instead bombards the real user with repeated MFA push notifications until one gets approved out of fatigue, not consent.
4. Either path ends the same way: the adversary is now authenticating with a legitimate account, no exploit, no malware, through the front door.

Mapping that without jumping straight to a label: step 1 and 2 are social engineering directed at a third party (the help desk) to subvert an authentication mechanism, not a technical exploit. Step 3, the push-bombing path, matches [T1621, Multi-Factor Authentication Request Generation](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1621/), confirmed under the **Credential Access** tactic: the adversary already has a password and is specifically trying to clear the second factor. Step 4, in both paths, is [T1078.004, Valid Accounts: Cloud Accounts](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1078/004/), and *now* Jordan's four-tactic table earns its keep: this specific use is Initial Access, because it's how Scattered Spider got in, not Persistence or Privilege Escalation, because the evidence here doesn't show either of those yet. If the same account gets used two weeks later to survive a password rotation, that's a second, separate mapping, Persistence, from the same technique ID, backed by new evidence, not assumed from the first.

Notice what the help-desk social-engineering step *doesn't* cleanly map to: it's real, it's documented, it's the actual initial lever Scattered Spider pulls, and it's closer to a pretext than a named technique with a clean ID. That's a deliberately honest thing to show rather than paper over. Not every sentence in a threat report corresponds to exactly one ATT&CK technique, and forcing one is worse than leaving a gap and noting it.

## A Workflow for Mapping Raw Evidence

Generalized from the walkthrough above, into steps that hold up against a report that isn't this tidy:

1. **Write down what the evidence actually shows**, separate from what you suspect. "An account was reset after a help desk call" is evidence. "Scattered Spider attacked us" is a conclusion, and it's not step one.
2. **Identify the behavior**, not the actor. What did whoever did this actually do, mechanically?
3. **Map the behavior to a technique**, checking the technique's actual description against your evidence rather than against a title that sounds close.
4. **Use a sub-technique** only when the technique page defines one that matches, `T1078.004` here, not every technique has this extra level.
5. **Identify the tactic from the objective the evidence supports**, not from the technique's full list of possible tactics. T1078 lists four; this incident's evidence supports exactly one of them at a time.
6. **Keep the procedure-level detail separate and preserved.** "Help desk call, personal details used as pretext, MFA re-enrolled to attacker-controlled device" is what makes this incident investigable later. It's not part of the technique ID, but losing it loses the thing a responder actually needs.
