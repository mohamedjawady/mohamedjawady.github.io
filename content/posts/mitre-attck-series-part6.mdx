**The crux of this part:** given a technique that already has a working detection, what does a listed mitigation actually commit a defender to, and how do you decide, for each one, whether it's worth the operational cost of turning it on versus relying on detection alone?

## T1021.002's Four Real Mitigations

Checked live against the current [T1021.002 technique page](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1021/002/), exactly four mitigations are listed, the same technique Part 5 built AN1468 for:

<MitigationD3FENDMap />

Notice the spread. M1026, privileged account management, is close to a free lunch: don't let domain users sit in the local Administrators group across machines, don't allow remote admin logons with local credentials. It costs process discipline, not functionality. M1035, disabling administrative shares outright, is the opposite: it would eliminate the exact pathway C0029 used, and it would also break legitimate remote administration, software deployment tooling, and backup jobs that plenty of organizations still depend on `ADMIN$`/`C$` for. Same technique, same mitigations list, wildly different cost to actually turn on.

This is the detail a flat "technique: mitigated" checkbox erases completely. A mitigation existing on a page is a menu, not a prescription. The actual work is figuring out which items on that menu your environment can afford.

## D3FEND: Naming the Specific Thing, Not Just the Category

ATT&CK's mitigations are broad categories, "Privileged Account Management" covers a lot of ground. [D3FEND](https://d3fend.mitre.org/), a separate MITRE knowledge graph, exists to get more specific about the actual defensive technique underneath a broad mitigation label. Checked directly against D3FEND's own ATT&CK-mitigation mapping:

- **M1037, Filter Network Traffic** maps to **D3-NI, Network Isolation**: preventing hosts from reaching non-essential network resources, the specific mechanism behind "restrict SMB with the host firewall."
- **M1026, Privileged Account Management** maps to **D3-LAM (Local Account Monitoring)** and **D3-DAM (Domain Account Monitoring)**: watching for unauthorized local and domain account activity, not just the policy of restricting privilege, the technical means of noticing when the policy's being violated.
- **M1027, Password Policies** maps to **D3-SPP, Strong Password Policy**: directly, unsurprisingly, since the mitigation and the D3FEND technique describe almost the same thing from two angles.

What D3FEND adds isn't new information, it's vocabulary precision. "We do privileged account management" is a sentence that could mean almost anything. "We do local and domain account monitoring (D3-LAM, D3-DAM) specifically" is a sentence that names an actual technical capability someone could be asked to demonstrate. It's worth being honest about D3FEND's limits too: a mapping existing between an ATT&CK mitigation and a D3FEND technique is not proof either one is implemented in a given environment, any more than a Detection Strategy existing on a technique page proved a detection worked back in Part 5. Same discipline, different layer.

## Prevent What You Can Afford, Detect What You Can't

Jordan's instinct in the dialogue above, that prevention should just be the obviously cheaper choice, is the right instinct pointed at the wrong variable. The real cost of a mitigation isn't implementing it, it's everything downstream that depended on the thing being allowed. A practical way to sort the four options above, and most mitigation decisions generally:

| Question | If yes | T1021.002 example |
|---|---|---|
| **Does turning this on break anything that currently works?** | Weigh the operational cost seriously before committing | M1035 (disable admin shares) almost certainly yes, in most environments |
| **Is the cost close to zero and the benefit real?** | Just do it, no tradeoff debate needed | M1026, M1027: process and policy changes, not functionality removed |
| **Does this reduce the blast radius if prevention fails anyway?** | Worth doing even alongside detection | M1026 again: limits damage even if an account does get compromised |
| **Can the behavior be observed reliably if you don't block it?** | Lean on detection instead of forcing prevention | SMB admin share access can be, which is exactly why Part 5's AN1468 is a reasonable primary control here |

Applied to T1021.002 specifically: M1026 and M1027 are close to unconditional, do them regardless of whether AN1468 exists, because the cost is near zero and the benefit (smaller blast radius, harder credential reuse) holds up either way. M1037, network-level SMB filtering, is a worthwhile middle option in most environments, segmenting which hosts can even reach `ADMIN$` on which other hosts, without eliminating the capability outright. M1035, disabling administrative shares entirely, is the one that actually deserves the debate Jordan wanted to skip: it's the strongest prevention on the list and the most likely to break something real, which is exactly the scenario where Part 5's detection earns its keep as the primary control instead of a backup to a prevention that never got safely turned on.

The general shape, independent of this specific technique: mitigate what can be made true consistently, without breaking the organization. Detect what can't be blocked without unacceptable cost. For anything with a large enough blast radius if it fails, M1026-style access restriction being a good example, do both, because detection catches what prevention misses and restricted blast radius limits the damage detection didn't catch in time.
