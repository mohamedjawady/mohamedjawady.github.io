**The crux of this part:** what's the difference between a technique that's mapped, one that's claimed as covered, and one that's actually been validated, and what tools turn that distinction into a repeatable habit instead of a one-time audit?

## T1021.002, Traced Through Its Own Coverage States

<CoverageJourney />

That three-word progression, **Unknown, Claimed, Validated**, is worth being precise about, because the gap between the middle and the last one is where most security programs quietly live without noticing:

- **Unknown** means exactly that: the team hasn't established whether the required visibility or detection capability exists. Not "probably fine," not "likely covered by the EDR," actually unknown.
- **Claimed** means a detection, a tool, or a vendor dashboard says the technique is covered, but nobody has recently produced evidence that claim is true. This is the state most coverage spreadsheets quietly live in, and the most dangerous one to mistake for finished, because it looks identical to Validated from the outside.
- **Validated** means an authorized test produced evidence: the telemetry was generated, it reached the platform, the detection logic matched, and the resulting alert carried enough context to act on. Part 5's five-step test is what moved T1021.002 from Claimed to Validated, not the existence of AN1468 on the technique page.

A detection rule existing, a vendor marketing slide, or a technique appearing on a Navigator layer are all, at most, a Claimed-level data point. None of them are evidence by themselves. The discipline this whole series has been building, one layer at a time, is just this: don't let a Claimed-level artifact get treated as Validated-level evidence, anywhere in the chain, whether that's a technique mapping, a group attribution, a detection strategy, or a mitigation.

## The Real Tools, Briefly

A handful of tools, all free and maintained by the Center for Threat-Informed Defense, exist specifically to make this workflow repeatable instead of a one-off exercise redone from scratch every time:

| Tool | What it's actually for |
|---|---|
| **[ATT&CK Navigator](https://mitre-attack.github.io/attack-navigator/)** | Visualize and annotate techniques in layers, exactly the kind of Unknown/Claimed/Validated layer this part is built around. |
| **[ATT&CK Powered Suit](https://ctid.mitre.org/projects/attack-powered-suit/)** | A browser extension that helps translate an unstructured CTI report, like the Part 4 C0029 writeup, into candidate technique mappings, exported straight into Navigator. |
| **[Technique Inference Engine (TIE)](https://ctid.mitre.org/projects/technique-inference-engine/)** | A machine-learning tool that suggests plausibly related techniques from a partial observed set. Useful for widening an investigation; its suggestions are hypotheses, not confirmed behavior. |
| **[ATT&CK Workbench](https://ctid.mitre.org/projects/attck-workbench)** | A place to preserve internal notes, detection designs, and validation results so the next review doesn't start from zero, this post, structurally, is doing by hand what Workbench is built to do systematically. |
| **[ATT&CK Sync](https://ctid.mitre.org/projects/attack-sync/)** | Helps review what changed between ATT&CK releases. Given how much of this series turned out to hinge on catching recent changes, the Stealth/Defense Impairment split in Part 3, Data Sources' deprecation in Part 5, a tool built specifically to catch exactly that kind of drift is worth having on a schedule, not just reached for when something breaks. |

None of these tools produce Validated-level evidence by themselves. Navigator shows you what you believe. TIE suggests what might be related. Workbench remembers what you found. Sync tells you what changed. The actual validation step, Part 5's five-step test, still has to happen outside all of them, against your own environment's real telemetry.

## A Backlog Instead of a Dashboard

The practical output of a coverage review shouldn't be a bigger spreadsheet. It should be a small number of specific, assigned actions. Applied to this series' own running example, a review might produce:

1. **T1021.002: Validated.** AN1468 tested and confirmed (Part 5). Maintain; retest if the environment's logging configuration changes.
2. **M1026, M1027: Done, low effort.** Policy changes confirmed in place (Part 6). No further action unless an audit finds drift.
3. **M1035: Open decision, not a gap.** Deliberately not enabled due to operational dependency on admin shares; documented as an accepted risk with AN1468 as the compensating control, not a forgotten TODO.
4. **Related techniques from C0029's full 28-technique list (Part 4): Unknown.** This series only ever validated one of them. The other 27 are exactly where T1021.002 started, before anyone had done this same five-step process for them specifically.

That last line is the honest note to end a series like this one. Three techniques' worth of deep, validated work out of one campaign's 28 isn't a failure of the method, it's what the method actually looks like applied for real: slow, specific, and honest about what hasn't been done yet rather than inflated into a dashboard that implies more than three techniques' worth of confidence.

## Closing the Loop

Back to Part 1's loop: Understand, Improve, Test and Measure, Learn and Repeat. This series walked that loop once, all the way around, on one real campaign and one real technique inside it. C0029 was the Understand step (Parts 1 and 4). AN1468 and the four mitigations were Improve (Parts 5 and 6). The five-step validation process was Test and Measure (Part 5). And this part, the honest backlog, the 27 still-Unknown techniques, is Learn and Repeat: not a finish line, the input to doing this again, for the next technique, with slightly better judgment about where the real work actually is than the team had at the start of Part 1.

That's the whole argument this series has been making one layer at a time: ATT&CK is a vocabulary precise enough to support that loop, not a checklist that completes it for you. The framework can tell you what a tactic is, what a technique commits you to, which tools exist to help. It can't do the five-step test. It can't decide whether M1035 is worth your organization's operational cost. It can't tell you, honestly, whether "covered" means mapped, claimed, or validated on your own dashboard right now. That part was always going to be the actual work. ATT&CK just makes sure, when you do it, you and the next person doing the same work are using the same words to describe it.
